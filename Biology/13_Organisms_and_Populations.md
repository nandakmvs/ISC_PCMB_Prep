# Chapter 13: Organisms and Populations

## 1. Exhaustive Theory & Precise ISC Terminology

### Organism and Its Environment
* Ecology is essentially concerned with four levels of biological organisation: **organisms, populations, communities, and biomes**.

**Major Abiotic Factors**
1. **Temperature**: Most ecologically relevant environmental factor. Dictates the latitudinal/altitudinal distribution of organisms.
   * **Eurythermal**: Organisms that can tolerate and thrive in a wide range of temperatures.
   * **Stenothermal**: Organisms restricted to a narrow range of temperatures.
2. **Water**: Next most important factor. Salinity (salt concentration) is crucial for aquatic life.
   * **Euryhaline**: Tolerant to a wide range of salinities.
   * **Stenohaline**: Restricted to a narrow range of salinities. Many freshwater animals cannot live for long in seawater and vice versa because of the osmotic problems.
3. **Light**: Necessary for photosynthesis. Plants may be adapted to low light (sciophytes) or high light (heliophytes). Photoperiodism affects flowering in plants and foraging/reproduction in animals.
4. **Soil**: Properties like grain size, aggregation, pH, and mineral composition determine the water holding capacity and dictate the type of vegetation (and thus the animals).

**Responses to Abiotic Factors**
Organisms try to maintain the constancy of its internal environment (a process called **homeostasis**).
1. **Regulate**: Maintain constant body temperature (thermoregulation) and constant osmotic concentration (osmoregulation) by physiological/behavioral means. Only mammals, birds, and a few lower vertebrates can do this.
2. **Conform**: The majority of animals (99%) and nearly all plants cannot maintain a constant internal environment. Their body temperature/osmotic concentration changes with the ambient conditions.
3. **Migrate**: The organism can move away temporarily from the stressful habitat to a more hospitable area and return when stressful period is over (e.g., Siberian birds at Keoladeo National Park, Bharatpur).
4. **Suspend**:
   * Bacteria, fungi, and lower plants form thick-walled spores to survive unfavorable conditions.
   * Animals may undergo **Hibernation** (winter sleep, e.g., bears) or **Aestivation** (summer sleep to avoid heat and desiccation, e.g., snails, fish).
   * **Diapause**: A stage of suspended development in many zooplanktons under unfavorable conditions.

**Adaptations**
* Any attribute of the organism (morphological, physiological, behavioral) that enables the organism to survive and reproduce in its habitat.
* **Morphological**: Thick cuticle, sunken stomata, leaves reduced to spines in desert plants (*Opuntia*).
* **Allen’s Rule**: Mammals from colder climates generally have shorter ears and limbs to minimize heat loss.
* **Physiological**: Altitude sickness occurs at high altitudes ($>3500$m) due to low atmospheric pressure (low oxygen). Body compensates by increasing RBC production, decreasing binding affinity of hemoglobin, and increasing breathing rate.
* **Behavioral**: Desert lizards bask in the sun and absorb heat when their body temperature drops below the comfort zone, but move into shade when the ambient temperature starts increasing.

### Populations
**Population Attributes**
* **Birth rate (Natality)** and **Death rate (Mortality)**: Expressed as per capita births/deaths.
* **Sex Ratio**: Ratio of males to females in a population.
* **Age Pyramids**: Graphic representation of the age structure of a population (Pre-reproductive, Reproductive, Post-reproductive). Shapes indicate growth status: Expanding (triangular), Stable (bell-shaped), Declining (urn-shaped).

**Population Growth**
Population density ($N$) at time $t+1$ is given by: $N_{t+1} = N_t + [(B + I) - (D + E)]$
* $B$ = Births, $I$ = Immigration, $D$ = Deaths, $E$ = Emigration.
1. **Exponential Growth**: Occurs when resources (food and space) are unlimited. Results in a J-shaped curve. Equation: $dN/dt = rN$. ($r$ = intrinsic rate of natural increase).
2. **Logistic Growth**: Occurs when resources are limited (realistic condition). Results in an S-shaped (Sigmoid) curve. Population reaches a **Carrying Capacity ($K$)**. Equation: $dN/dt = rN[(K - N) / K]$. Also known as Verhulst-Pearl Logistic Growth.

**Life History Variation**
* Darwinian fitness (high $r$ value). Some organisms breed only once in their lifetime (Pacific salmon fish, bamboo) while others breed many times (most birds and mammals).
* Some produce a large number of small-sized offspring (Oysters, pelagic fishes) while others produce a small number of large-sized offspring (birds, mammals).

**Population Interactions**
1. **Predation (+, -)**: Predator benefits, prey is harmed. Predators act as conduits for energy transfer and keep prey populations under control. Prey species have evolved defenses (camouflage, poisonous chemicals like cardiac glycosides in *Calotropis*).
2. **Competition (-, -)**: Interaction where fitness of one species is significantly lower in the presence of another.
   * **Gause’s ‘Competitive Exclusion Principle’**: Two closely related species competing for the same resources cannot co-exist indefinitely and the competitively inferior one will be eliminated eventually.
   * **Resource Partitioning**: Co-existence by choosing different times for feeding or different foraging patterns (e.g., MacArthur's warblers).
3. **Parasitism (+, -)**: Parasite benefits, host is harmed. Parasites may be **Ectoparasites** (ticks, lice, copepods, *Cuscuta*) or **Endoparasites** (liver fluke, *Plasmodium*).
   * **Brood Parasitism**: Birds (e.g., cuckoo/koel) lay eggs in the nest of its host (crow).
4. **Commensalism (+, 0)**: One benefits, other is unaffected. E.g., Orchid growing as an epiphyte on a mango branch; Barnacles on a whale; Cattle egret and grazing cattle; Sea anemone and clownfish.
5. **Mutualism (+, +)**: Both benefit. E.g., Lichens (fungi + algae/cyanobacteria); Mycorrhizae (fungi + plant roots); Wasp and fig tree pollination; Orchid *Ophrys* and bumblebees (sexual deceit).
6. **Amensalism (-, 0)**: One is harmed, other is unaffected. E.g., Penicillium secreting penicillin that kills bacteria.

## 2. Step-by-Step Derivations & Mechanisms

### Logistic Growth Equation Breakdown
1. Assume a population has size $N$. Let $r$ be the intrinsic rate of increase.
2. Without limits, the growth rate is $dN/dt = rN$ (Exponential).
3. In nature, environments have a maximum capacity they can support, called **Carrying Capacity ($K$)**.
4. The term $(K-N)/K$ acts as the environmental resistance fraction.
   * When $N$ is small, $(K-N)/K \approx 1$, so growth is exponential.
   * As $N$ approaches $K$, $(K-N)/K$ approaches 0, so growth stops ($dN/dt = 0$).
5. The combined equation is: $dN/dt = rN[(K - N) / K]$.

## 4. "Avoid the Trap" & Distinction Tables

### Trap Warnings
* **Brood Parasitism vs True Parasitism**: In brood parasitism (cuckoo and crow), the parasite does not consume the host's body tissues for food; it tricks the host into raising its offspring. It is still (+, -).
* **Migration vs Emigration**: Migration is a temporary, cyclical movement. Emigration is permanent one-way movement out of a population.
* **Allen's vs Bergmann's Rule**: Allen's rule specifies *appendages/limbs* are shorter in cold climates.

*Answer:* (C)

**Problem 2 (Subjective/ISC):** Differentiate between exponential and logistic growth models. (3 marks)
**Step-by-Step Method:**
1. **Resource Availability**: Exponential growth occurs when resources (food, space) are unlimited. Logistic growth occurs when resources are limited.
2. **Curve Shape**: Exponential growth produces a J-shaped curve. Logistic growth produces a Sigmoid (S-shaped) curve.
3. **Equation**: Exponential is represented by $dN/dt = rN$. Logistic is represented by $dN/dt = rN[(K - N) / K]$, where $K$ is the carrying capacity.
4. **Realism**: Logistic is more realistic in nature as no population can grow exponentially forever.
